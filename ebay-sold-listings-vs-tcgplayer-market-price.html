eBay Sold Listings vs TCGplayer Market Price: Which Is More Accurate?

Look up any popular Pokémon card on two different sites and you'll often see two different prices. eBay sold listings show what buyers actually paid; TCGplayer market price shows a computed average from its own marketplace. Both are useful — but they answer different questions, and knowing which one to trust in each situation saves you from overpaying or underpricing your own cards.

How eBay sold listings work

On eBay, anyone can ask any price. What matters is the Sold items filter, which shows completed sales with their actual final prices. This is the raw, unfiltered market: auctions with sniping wars, Buy It Now deals, overgraded cards that sold anyway, and the occasional mislabeled listing where someone got a steal.

The strength of sold listings is granularity. You can see exactly which printing, condition, and grading sold for what, down to individual sales from the last few days. For a card that just spiked — a tournament win, a ban-list change, an influencer pull — eBay solds are the first place the movement shows up.

The weakness is noise. Outliers happen constantly: one buyer overpaid at 2 a.m., one listing had the wrong set in the title, one "sold" price was actually an unpaid auction. A single sale tells you almost nothing; the trend across a dozen recent sales tells you a lot.

How TCGplayer market price works

TCGplayer's market price is a single computed number: roughly the average sale price of that card in each condition, calculated from completed sales on TCGplayer's own marketplace over a recent window. It smooths out the noise for you, updating as new sales come in.

Its strength is convenience and consistency. One number, comparable across every card, based on a marketplace where sellers are mostly established TCG retailers and the condition standards are relatively uniform. For a quick sanity check on a modern card's value, it's the fastest single figure available.

Its weakness is that it's a black box with a blind spot. The exact formula isn't public, it reflects only TCGplayer's own sales (which skew toward condition-graded singles from shops), and it can lag behind fast-moving markets by days. For vintage, graded, or sealed items, its data is much thinner than eBay's.

When they disagree — and why

Disagreement between the two sources usually means something specific. Here are the common patterns:

eBay solds run hotter than TCGplayer. This is typical for graded cards, high-end chase cards, and anything riding a hype wave: TCGplayer's sellers are mostly shops moving raw singles, while eBay hosts the auction market where collectors bid up PSA slabs and rare pulls.

TCGplayer runs hotter than eBay. This happens with played-condition cards and bulk-ish rares, where TCGplayer's near-mint shop listings set a higher anchor while eBay solds show buyers scooping up the cheaper copies.

One of them has almost no data. For obscure printings — foreign-language cards, promos, misprints — one source may show two ancient sales while the other has fresh ones. The source with more recent sales wins, every time.

Which to use when

  • Pricing cards you're selling: check both, then price against eBay solds of the same condition — that's what buyers are actually paying right now.
  • Buying singles: TCGplayer market price is a fine anchor for modern near-mint singles, but glance at eBay solds to make sure you aren't overpaying the shop premium.
  • Trading with another collector: agree on one source before the trade. Mixed sources are how trade disputes start.
  • Graded cards (PSA/CGC): lean on eBay solds — TCGplayer's graded data is thin and the premium lives in auctions.
  • Checking a binder fast: use an aggregator. CardHawk, the free Android card scanner, pulls market pricing from PriceCharting — which itself aggregates eBay sold data — so you get the eBay side of the story without digging through listings one card at a time.

The tiebreaker rule

When the two sources disagree and you need a single number, use this: the most recent comparable sales are the market. An average of the last ten eBay solds in the same condition beats any computed market price for that specific card, right now. Averages are guides; actual sales are verdicts.

One habit makes this whole comparison reliable: always match the exact printing and condition — a secret rare and its regular rare sibling can differ 50x, and played vs near-mint changes everything. Ignore the extreme outliers too: the highest and lowest solds are usually mistakes, not the market.

FAQ

Frequently asked questions

Is TCGplayer market price accurate?

It's a reasonable average of sales on TCGplayer's own marketplace — accurate for modern near-mint singles, less reliable for graded, vintage, or sealed items where its sales data is thin.

Why is the eBay sold price different from TCGplayer?

They measure different marketplaces. eBay solds include auctions, impulse buys, and graded slabs; TCGplayer reflects shop sales of mostly raw singles. Different buyer pools, different prices.

Can I just average the two?

Only if both sources have enough recent sales. If one source has twenty solds this week and the other has two from last month, trust the deeper data — don't average fresh and stale.
