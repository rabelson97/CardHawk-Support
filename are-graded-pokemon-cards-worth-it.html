Are Graded Pokémon Cards Worth It? When Grading Pays Off — and When It Doesn't

Grading can turn a $20 raw card into a $200 slab — or turn a $20 card into a $10 card after fees. Here's how to decide whether PSA, CGC, or BGS grading is worth it for your cards.

Every collector has stared at a clean-looking card and wondered: should I send this in? A PSA 10 can sell for several times the raw price, and graded cards are easier to sell. But grading isn't free, it isn't fast, and it doesn't always pay. The difference between a smart submission and a wasted fee comes down to three things: the card's raw value, its likely grade, and what the graded version actually sells for.

What grading actually does

When you submit a card to PSA, CGC, or Beckett (BGS), a professional grader authenticates it, scores its condition on a 1–10 scale, and seals it in a tamper-evident case. That label proves the card is real and settles arguments about condition — a buyer who can't tell near-mint from lightly played will pay more for a card a third party already scored. That's the whole premium: trust, plus the scarcity of high grades. But grading doesn't create value out of nothing. A common card in a slab is still a common card; the premium only exists when collectors compete for that card in that grade.

What grading costs

Budget for more than the per-card fee. PSA's economy service typically runs around $25 per card for cards valued under a few hundred dollars, with higher tiers for more valuable cards; CGC and BGS charge roughly similar amounts. Add insured shipping both ways, and the wait: economy turnarounds are commonly measured in weeks to a few months. A single card can easily cost $35–50 all-in by the time it comes home. That math is the whole decision: if the card is worth $15 raw, grading it at $40 means you need a grade that sells for at least $55 just to break even.

When grading pays off

Grading is usually worth it when several of these are true at once:

  • The card is already valuable raw. A $100+ raw card has room for a graded premium to cover the fee. A $5 card almost never does.
  • The card is likely to grade high. Pack-fresh cards with sharp corners, clean edges, a scratch-free surface, and well-centered printing earn 9s and 10s. Whitening on the back edges or a print line across the holo means a lower grade — and a smaller premium.
  • High grades of that card are scarce or in demand. Vintage holos, popular modern chase cards like special illustration rares, and iconic cards (Charizard, Umbreon, Rayquaza) command the biggest jumps from raw to PSA 10.

When grading is a waste of money

Skip grading when the card is low-value, visibly played, or a common pull. A PSA 8 of a $10 card often sells for less than the raw card plus the grading fee — you paid to make your card worth less. The same goes for bulk: grading ten copies of a $3 card is ten fees chasing a premium that doesn't exist. And beware the "grade everything" impulse after a good pull. A modern ultra rare that sells raw for $30 might fetch $60 as a PSA 10, but if it comes back a 9, it might sell for $35 — barely above raw, after you paid $40 to find out. Only grade if the expected graded sale price, minus all fees and shipping, beats the raw price by a margin you'd be happy with. If the answer depends on hitting a perfect 10, you're gambling, not investing.

PSA vs CGC vs BGS: does the company matter?

Yes, mostly for resale. PSA slabs generally command the highest resale prices for Pokémon cards, especially at grades 9 and 10 — it's the default buyers search for. CGC is a strong second choice, popular for its subgrades, which break the score down into centering, corners, edges, and surface. BGS is respected too, and a BGS 10 "Black Label" (perfect subgrades) can outsell everything — but it's rare, and BGS 9s and 9.5s often sell below PSA 9s. For a card you're submitting to sell, PSA is the safe default.

Pre-grade your cards before you submit

Graders score four things, and you can check all of them at home with good light and a magnifier:

  1. Centering: compare the borders on the front and back. Off-center printing caps the grade before anything else is considered.
  2. Corners: look for whitening or softening, especially on the back corners. Even tiny wear shows up under grading.
  3. Edges: look for nicks or silvering on the foil border.
  4. Surface: tilt the card under a lamp and look for scratches, print lines, and dents.

If a card fails any of these checks, it probably isn't a 10 — and knowing that before you pay the fee is the whole point of checking.

Check the raw price first

The easiest mistake in grading is submitting a card without knowing what it's worth raw. If the raw price is $12, no grade is going to save the submission. Before you send anything in, identify the exact card — set, number, and finish — and check its current market price alongside recent sold listings for the graded version. Grading is a tool, not a prize. Used on the right cards, it protects your card, proves its condition, and pays for itself. Used on everything else, it's an expensive plastic case.

FAQ

Frequently asked questions

How much does it cost to grade a Pokémon card?

Expect roughly $25–50 per card at economy service with PSA, CGC, or BGS, plus insured shipping both ways — so $35–50 all-in for a single card. Higher-value cards require higher-priced service tiers.

How long does PSA grading take?

Economy submissions commonly take several weeks to a few months. Faster turnaround tiers exist but cost significantly more per card.

Is a PSA 9 worth grading?

Sometimes. A PSA 9 of a popular or valuable card usually sells for more than the raw version, but the premium is much smaller than a PSA 10's. For low-value cards, a 9 often doesn't cover the grading fee.

Should I grade modern cards or only vintage?

Both can be worth grading if the card is valuable, clean, and in demand — modern chase cards like special illustration rares often grade well and sell fast. The deciding factor is the graded sale price minus fees, not the card's age.
